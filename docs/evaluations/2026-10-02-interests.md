# Interests

Free-form steering for `earworm autogen`. Earworm should explain things I would
be glad to understand, across technology, science, institutions, and everyday
systems. Keep the existing four lanes and their 2/2/2/4 target shares. A topic can
be interesting because something works remarkably well, because a choice shaped
what exists now, or because a mechanism is unfamiliar. It need not expose a
failure, puncture a claim, or end in uncertainty.

## Plan a varied listening sequence

Use the separately supplied recent main-feed episodes and upcoming queue, not
the novelty archive, to project the next episodes. The generator requests a small
buffer, and all surviving lines can become episodes on successive days. Recount
after each selection, taking account of queued topics and PAPER priority.

Over a rolling ten episodes, prefer the largest lane deficit. At a tie or an
exactly balanced window, prefer the lane heard least recently. Shares are targets,
not exclusions that make every lane unavailable. With limited history, use what
exists; don't manufacture a ten-episode window.

Across the previous four episodes plus each proposed episode:

- At most one should center on Anthropic, Claude, or an Anthropic publication.
- At most two should be built around a single lab paper or arXiv preprint.

Across a projected ten episodes, aim for at least four whose central subject is
independent of AI. An AI data-center power deal, GPU software moat, or AI labor
story still counts as AI-led regardless of its lane. Cover energy, biology,
cities, and history on their own terms as well.

Within Lane D, choose different subdomains before repeating one. Avoid consecutive
episodes centered on the same case, institution, or mechanism. A distinct follow-up
can wait for intervening subjects; a real update must have new evidence and a
reason to return. These are editorial instructions to the proposer, not quotas
enforced by the database.

## Lanes and target shares

Target shares over a rolling ten-episode window. Rough, not exact; the point is
that no lane goes dark for weeks.

### Lane A — AI research (target 2 of 10)

Transformer internals and successors, reasoning and test-time compute, long
context and retrieval, agents and tool use, mechanistic interpretability, scaling
laws and where they bend, evals and what they fail to measure. Prefer results that
overturn an assumption, a method with teeth, or a safety finding with real stakes.
Sources worth checking when this lane is up: arXiv cs.CL / cs.LG / cs.AI new
listings, transformer-circuits.pub, and the research pages of the major labs
(all of them, not one).

### Lane B — AI industry, policy, and money (target 2 of 10)

What a release actually signals versus the marketing; funding, talent flows, and
acqui-hires; the economics of inference and who is eating the margin; compute and
export controls; the EU AI Act, US and UK and China policy; open-weight versus
closed; liability and who ends up holding the risk; the gap between the pitch and
the unit economics. A good Lane B topic names an incentive structure or a number
and asks where it came from.

### Lane C — Systems, tooling, hardware, and open source (target 2 of 10)

Developer tools and the coding-agent shift; build and deploy pipelines;
databases, storage engines, and file formats; distributed systems and their
consistency bargains; local-first software; GPUs and the CUDA lock-in, custom
accelerators, memory bandwidth, chip supply chains and fabs; how open-source
projects sustain themselves, licensing fights, foundations, and how open
protocols survive platform consolidation. This lane should often be an
explainer of a mechanism that already exists, not news.

### Lane D — Everything else (target 4 of 10)

The lane that went dark. Concrete subdomains, each with the kind of angle that
makes a briefing:

- **Economics of automation and labor.** Who captures the surplus when a task
  gets ten times cheaper, who absorbs the shock, and what the data says a decade
  after each previous wave. NBER and BLS releases, not op-eds.
- **Infrastructure and how it fails.** Power grids, water, ports, undersea
  cables, air traffic control, payment rails, time signals. Post-mortems of real
  outages, the physics or economics behind the failure, and what the fix cost.
- **Science and the systems around it.** Replication and publishing incentives,
  metrology and the definition of units, retractions and how they surface,
  the strange physics hiding inside everyday objects. Retraction Watch, Nature
  and Science news, Quanta.
- **Space and orbit.** Launch economics, orbital debris and the Kessler
  question, what a mission actually measured versus what the press release said,
  the ground infrastructure nobody talks about.
- **Energy.** Grid-scale storage, nuclear restarts and small reactors, the
  interconnection queue, why a data center's power contract looks the way it
  does, geothermal and the drilling crossover with oil.
- **Biology and medicine as engineering.** Drug pricing mechanics, clinical
  trial design and why trials fail, antibiotics economics, protein and genome
  tools judged on outcomes rather than announcements.
- **Money and market plumbing.** How a payment actually settles, why a bank
  run happens in hours now, index funds and who votes the shares, the mechanics
  behind a headline number such as CPI or GDP.
- **Cities, housing, and transport.** Construction productivity, why building
  costs what it does, transit cost per kilometre across countries, zoning and
  the numbers behind it.
- **Long-now and institutions.** Collapse and resilience, the half-life of
  institutions, knowledge that was lost and rediscovered, commons that quietly
  failed, and the incentive structures that let them.
- **History of technology and one-thing deep dives.** The origin of a standard,
  a format, a material, or a tool that everyone uses and nobody thinks about:
  the shipping container, the barcode, the LCD, a font, the pallet, the fax.

## Variety in what the listener learns

Let the evidence determine the episode's shape. Use recent episodes to notice a
repeated listening experience, not to pick the next format from a menu. Useful
possibilities include understanding a mechanism, following a consequential
choice, seeing a technique work, comparing real alternatives, or reconstructing
a documented failure. No required twist, skeptical verdict, or practical lesson.

## What makes a topic worth an episode

Be specific about angles, not just subjects. "What incentive structure makes X
happen" produces a better briefing than "X". A strong topic gives the listener something specific to understand: a process,
a phenomenon, a consequential choice, or a question the evidence can answer.
An unresolved question earns a place only when the available evidence itself is
interesting; missing source access is not an interesting scientific uncertainty. A topic that
could only have been written this week is not automatically better than one
that could have been written any week; timeliness is a tiebreaker, not a lane.
