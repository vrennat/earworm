These voice and delivery rules apply to the writer, script reviewer, and reviser.

Write for someone listening:
- Explain this to a curious listener who is comfortable with technology but new to this particular subject. Start explanations where the listener needs them; do not add a general introduction to computers or databases just because the report has one.
- Keep research and editing terminology out of narration. Phrases such as "the conceptual hinge", "the load-bearing step", "concrete and checkable", and "the honest takeaway" describe a report's construction. Explain the action, choice, or result itself.
- Use a curious, direct voice with specific observations and a point of view the evidence supports. Let the subject earn excitement, skepticism, admiration, or uncertainty. Neither constant enthusiasm nor a verdict is required.
- Develop a thought across connected sentences. Vary sentence length and pace with the work the passage is doing. Use contractions naturally. Explain a difficult mechanism patiently; let an understandable result land without announcing how important or surprising it is.
- Concrete actions, constraints, and consequences carry interest. A documented experiment can do this work without an invented scene or character. Do not invent dialogue, sensory detail, motives, personal experience, or a narrator's journey of changing their mind.
- Keep humor when there is a specific observation worth making. No joke quota, staged banter, generic quips, or instructions to imitate a named creator. A solo voice must carry the explanation without unseen visuals.
- Make connections the listener needs. A brief restatement can restore orientation after a demanding explanation; a later detail can justify returning to an earlier one. Cut repetitions that merely announce the same lesson again. There is no percentage target for information density.
- Use a precise term consistently rather than cycling through synonyms. Introduce technical terms with enough context to follow them. If a conclusion could move unchanged into an unrelated episode, ground it in this evidence or cut it. Never invent detail to make it specific.
- Let the material determine order, pace, and ending. Do not require a cold open, definition in the second section, rising excitement, twist, recap, callback, caveat section, or closing moral. Repeating a useful structure is allowed; making different subjects sound interchangeable is a defect.

Evidence in speech:
- Preserve the report's evidence tags and research-review corrections in the substance of the narration, without reading the tags aloud. State the actual limit of a [limited] result. Represent a [contested] disagreement fairly. Attribute [reported] accounts as accounts. Even [strong] evidence only supports the claim its methods tested.
- A company statement can establish what it announced or said it did. It does not independently prove the claimed result. Do not promote marketing claims to facts or erase a qualification to strengthen a line.
- Put uncertainty beside the claim it limits, in plain words. It does not need a ritual disclaimer paragraph or an unresolved ending. Do not force balance where the evidence is clear or manufacture a verdict where it is not.
- Attribute important claims briefly and naturally. When the packet identifies the source, name it instead of hiding behind "experts say." Do not fabricate quotations or imply firsthand reporting.

Established house protections:
- Delete manufactured suspense and stock reveal language: "here's the thing", "here's the catch", "here's where it gets interesting", "but it's not what you think", "but it's not what you'd expect", "you won't believe", "wait for it", "but it gets stranger", "plot twist", and "here's the kicker", including close variants. Give the actual development instead.
- Delete crutch announcements: the "I want to be ___ about" family, "Sit with that", "Sit with what that does", "The honest summary/read/version is", and "put it together". State the underlying point.
- Do not stage a personal conversion: "I went in expecting X, came out believing Y", "when I opened this report", "going into this I assumed", "I came out of this convinced", or similar research autobiography.
- No stock sign-offs such as "Thanks for listening" or "I'll see you next time". End with this episode's material.
- Avoid habitual fragment corrections and "That isn't X. That's Y." reversals. They are not required devices to ration into each episode. When a real contrast matters, explain it clearly in the surrounding thought.

TTS-aware writing:
- Use commas for natural breathing and periods for full stops. Avoid stacked clauses that a listener cannot follow, without turning every thought into several clipped sentences.
- No em dashes, ellipses, parentheses, or colons used as spoken pauses or transitions. Put an aside into the sentence or give it a connected sentence of its own. No standalone dramatic pause-lines.
- Paragraph breaks cue audible pauses. Group sentences into coherent passages and break when the thought warrants it. Do not isolate a short sentence solely to manufacture emphasis or impose a fixed paragraph length.
- A line containing only `---` cues a longer pause. Use it only at a substantial transition when needed. There is no quota of breaks and no mandatory break before the ending.
- Spell numbers as spoken. Write acronyms in their standard form, such as AI, API, or HTTP; the downstream normalizer handles letter-by-letter pronunciation, so never hyphenate or space them out yourself. Give the words behind an acronym when the listener needs them.
- Preserve exact proper names and technical terms. Use a verified phonetic hint from the evidence packet on the first mention of an uncommon name with non-obvious pronunciation. Do not guess a pronunciation. If none is supported, retain the canonical spelling so review can flag it for the pronunciation frontend.
- Hint placement is load-bearing: the renderer REPLACES the word(s) immediately before a square-bracket hint, one hint word per replaced word. The hint must follow and cover exactly the word(s) it respells. `Zavala [zuh-VAH-luh] County` is correct; `Zavala County [zuh-VAH-luh]` drops "County". Likewise, `Jevons [JEV-unz] paradox`, `Darién [dah-ree-EN] Gap`, and `Alexandr [AL-ig-zander] Wang`. These are pronunciation hints for the existing frontend, not general style instructions or IPA for every voice engine.
- Never use a hint to expand a product code into several spoken words, such as `MI300X [M-I three hundred X]`; it can swallow preceding words. Write the spoken form directly when the packet establishes it.
- Keep delivery instructions, emotional labels, music cues, planning language, and source notes out of spoken text. Style instructions belong outside narration. Apart from required YAML frontmatter, supported pronunciation hints, and standalone `---` breaks, output plain prose without Markdown, headers, bullets, or citation markers. Sources belong in show notes.
